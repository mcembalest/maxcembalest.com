---
title: The Better Lesson
---

On the one hand, the Bitter Lesson states that mastery in any closed domain will be dominated by approaches that scale with one factor: computation.

On the other hand, there is a Better Lesson: public interest and understanding in a closed domain will be dominated by approaches that scale with two factors: mathematization and play.

Mathematization refers to the ability of players to choose a variety of useful mathematical models as a method of including/excluding aspects of a game in one's analytical treatment. Play refers to whatever one does to get a feel for a toy or a game, typically in a manner one enjoys. When mathematization and play are scaled up (whatever scale means in this context), there is no better way of increasing the public interest and understanding in a closed domain. This is not something I feel the need to prove, but rather believe is evident in the most popular games in the history of the world.

It is coherent to believe the Bitter Lesson—that scaling with computation works better than anything when it comes to problem solving in closed domains—and the Better Lesson—that getting people to take interest in a closed domain via mathematization and play and find it fun works better than anything.

The bitter lesson only applies to winning games. The better lesson applies to designing games. Suppose we can make the rules. What rules do we want?
